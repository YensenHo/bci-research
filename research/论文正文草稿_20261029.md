## 1. Introduction

Knowledge distillation (KD) compresses a large teacher model into a small student by transferring soft labels, and is widely assumed to improve the student whenever a well-trained teacher is available [Hinton 2015]. A growing body of work studies *when this assumption fails*: when the teacher is only slightly better than the student, distillation can be neutral or harmful [Cho 2019; Yuan 2020], and stronger teachers do not always transfer more [Strong Teacher Not Needed, 2026].

Critically, all of this prior work operates in a regime where the **teacher still performs above chance**. This is not an accident of the literature but a property of the data: in vision and language, training data are abundant, so a teacher trained on full labels essentially never falls below random guessing. The regime in which a teacher *collapses below chance*—and what distillation does there—has, to our knowledge, never been studied. A title-level and full-text search of "below chance", "below random", and "worse than chance" in the KD literature returns no prior work (see Section 2).

We argue that this regime is not a curiosity but a **first-order failure mode for low-SNR physiological signals**. Wearable brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) record EEG that is low in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), often from a single or few channels, under small sample sizes. Under these conditions a large teacher overfits and can collapse below chance, whereas the data are exactly where distillation is expected to help most (scarce labels, weak individual models).

Our contributions are:

1. **We identify and characterize a blind-spot failure mode of KD**: when the teacher's full-label accuracy falls below chance, distillation produces a large *negative* gain. In a sleep-stage EEG task (5 classes, single channel, 1,000 samples, additive noise) the teacher collapses to 10.1% (below the 20% chance), and distillation gain drops to **−32.17 pp** (two-seed mean), with the knowledge-transfer term K becoming strongly negative (−17.15 pp).

2. **We locate the three conditions** under which a teacher collapses below chance, each established by a controlled contrast: (i) task unsolvability (sleep 5-class single-channel collapses, whereas motor-imagery 4-class single-channel does not); (ii) low SNR (σ=0.5 does not collapse, σ=1.0 does); (iii) small sample size (1,000 collapses, 11,076 does not).

3. **We show a critical phase transition**: the sign of the distillation gain flips precisely when the teacher's accuracy crosses the chance level (from +7.76 pp to −32.17 pp across a noise sweep, two-seed stable).

4. We connect this to a **2×2 phase diagram** of distillation gain under noise (four quadrants, all two-seed verified) and to the mechanism: the knowledge-transfer term K follows sign(K) = sign(teacher − chance).

Our findings are practically relevant for on-device deployment of BCIs: before distilling a model for a low-SNR, hard, small-sample task, one should check whether the teacher is likely to fall below chance—in which case distillation is not merely useless but actively harmful.

## 3. Method

**Setup.** We use EEG-TCNet [Ingolfsson 2020], with a teacher (ELU, ~35K params) and a student (ReLU, ~9.5K params), identical architecture across two scales. Each experiment trains four models: (i) teacher on full labels; (ii) student on full labels; (iii) student on 30% hard labels ("hard"); (iv) student on 30% labels + teacher soft labels ("distilled"). Distillation gain = distilled − hard (pp). Temperature T=4.0, α=0.5, 100 epochs, two seeds (42/123) averaged; all key results are reported as two-seed means.

**K/R decomposition.** Following the label-smoothing view of KD [Yuan 2020], we decompose the gain into K (knowledge transfer) and R (regularization): K = distilled − label_smoothing, R = label_smoothing − hard, where label_smoothing is a student trained on 30% hard labels plus a *uniform* soft target (i.e., smoothing without teacher knowledge).

**Teacher-collapse definition.** A teacher is "collapsed" when its full-label validation accuracy falls below the chance level (random guessing, 1/C for C classes).

**Contrasts for the three conditions.** (i) *Task unsolvability*: sleep-stage classification (5 classes, single channel) vs. motor-imagery (4 classes, single channel). (ii) *SNR*: additive Gaussian noise σ ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0} injected after per-channel standardization. (iii) *Sample size*: 1,000 vs. 11,076 training samples.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 The teacher collapses below chance under three conditions

**Condition (i) — task unsolvability.** Figure/Table: on a sleep-stage task (5 classes, single channel, 1,000 samples, σ=1.0 noise) the teacher's full-label accuracy collapses to **10.1%**, below the 20% chance level. In contrast, on a motor-imagery task (4 classes, single channel, σ=1.0) the teacher stays at **28.7%**, above the 25% chance. A single channel alone is therefore *not* sufficient—the task itself must be near-unsolvable under the given information.

**Condition (ii) — low SNR.** A noise sweep on the sleep task (two-seed mean): at σ=0.5 the teacher is **21.7%** (above chance) and the gain is **+7.76 pp**; at σ=1.0 the teacher collapses to **10.1%** (below chance) and the gain flips to **−32.17 pp**. The collapse occurs within σ ∈ (0.5, 1.0].

**Condition (iii) — small sample size.** At σ=1.0, the teacher trained on **1,000** samples collapses to 10.1% (gain −32.17 pp), whereas the same teacher trained on **11,076** samples reaches **78.2%** (gain +8.92 pp). The 35K-parameter teacher overfits on 1,000 samples but learns well on 11,076—a direct causal contrast for the overfitting mechanism.

### 4.2 Critical phase transition at the chance level

The full noise sweep (two-seed) shows that the sign of the distillation gain flips **exactly where the teacher crosses chance**:

| σ | teacher (two-seed) | gain (two-seed) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 21.7% (> chance) | **+7.76** |
| 1.0 | 10.1% (< chance) | **−32.17** |
| 1.5 | 5.0% | −32.15 |
| 2.0 | 4.6% | −5.57 |

The transition is abrupt rather than gradual: once the teacher falls below chance, its soft labels become pure noise and the gain collapses. The severe-negative window is σ ∈ [1.0, 1.5], where the teacher is collapsed but the hard-label student still holds (52–65%); at σ=2.0 the hard student also collapses and the gain shrinks back to −5.57 pp.

### 4.3 Mechanism: K turns strongly negative

K/R decomposition of the collapsed case (σ=1.0, two-seed mean): **K = −17.15 pp**, **R = −15.03 pp**, total −32.17 pp. The teacher's soft labels are *worse* than a uniform target by 17 pp—knowledge transfer becomes *negative knowledge*. This sharpens the earlier finding that K is "always positive": K is positive **only while the teacher stays above chance**; below chance, K turns strongly negative. Equivalently, sign(K) = sign(teacher − chance).

### 4.4 The 2×2 phase diagram (context)

The collapse is the extreme (fourth) quadrant of a broader 2×2 phase diagram of distillation gain under noise, where the gain sign and magnitude are set by the position of the teacher's full-label accuracy and the hard-label accuracy relative to chance: (I) hard collapses + teacher informative → boom (+16.15 pp, BCIC 4-class); (II) hard does not collapse → mild (+9.38 pp); (III) hard collapses + teacher at chance → no gain (−0.18 pp); (IV) teacher collapses below chance → severe negative (−32.17 pp). All four quadrants are two-seed verified.
